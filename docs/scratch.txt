The Lighthouse Keeper's Last Night

For forty-one years, Elias had kept the lamp at Pellucid Point. He knew the moods of the sea the way other men knew the moods of their wives — by the smallest tells: the dull green it took on before a squall, the white-capped chop that meant the herring boats would come in early, the glassy stillness that lied about everything.

On the night the automation crew was due to arrive, the wind dropped. Elias climbed the iron stair one last time with a thermos of tea and a book he did not intend to read. The lamp turned. The beam swept out across the water, found nothing, came back. Swept, found, returned. It was a kind of breathing.

Around three in the morning he saw a light he did not recognize: small, low, the wrong color. It hung in the dark for the space of a single rotation and was gone. He waited for it. It did not come back.

In the morning the crew climbed the stairs with their toolboxes and their clipboards, and Elias shook their hands, and he carried his thermos and his unread book down to the dock where the supply boat was waiting. He did not tell them about the light. Some things, he thought, are owed to the dark.

He took the boat to the mainland, and for the rest of his life, on quiet evenings, he would stand at the kitchen window and watch the horizon for a long time, as if expecting something to answer.
